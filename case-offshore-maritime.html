Executive Overview and the 2026 Offshore Environment

Record-breaking energy production in the United States Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) reached an annual output of 714 million barrels. This surge has led to intense operational activity throughout the Gulf of Mexico and nearby waterways.

As deepwater drilling, platform refurbishing, and subsea intervention schedules increase, commercial mariners, platform technicians, and specialized contractors face heightened risks on the job.

When serious injuries occur offshore, navigating the legal claims process requires understanding a complex set of federal maritime laws and evolving case law.

The classification of an offshore worker—under the Jones Act, the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (LHWCA), or the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA)—determines the remedies available, standards for employer fault, and allowable financial compensation.

This analysis focuses on the current state of maritime injury law, including statutory rights, recent Fifth Circuit rulings, Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE) safety mandates, and essential legal strategies for injured offshore workers.

The Core Statutory Frameworks Governing Offshore Claims

Choosing the right statutory path after an offshore incident is vital. The applicable law decides whether a worker gets no-fault statutory benefits or can pursue full tort damages.

The Jones Act (46 U.S.C. § 30104)

The Jones Act offers clear statutory protection for seamen who suffer injuries while working.

Unlike workers' compensation plans for land-based employees, the Jones Act allows seamen to sue their employers for negligence.

To prove a case under the Jones Act, an injured mariner only needs to show that employer negligence contributed even slightly to the injury. This burden of proof is much lower than the standard in traditional common law.

Seamen also receive maintenance and cure, irrespective of fault. Maintenance gives a daily living allowance, while cure requires the vessel owner to cover reasonable and necessary medical expenses until the worker reaches maximal medical improvement.

Additionally, seamen can file strict liability claims for unseaworthiness, holding vessel owners responsible for ensuring that hulls, equipment, gear, and crews are fit for their intended use.

Recoverable damages under the Jones Act include lost wages, loss of earning capacity, medical costs, physical pain and suffering, and mental distress. Claims under the Jones Act are subject to a three-year statute of limitations.

The Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (LHWCA)

The LHWCA (33 U.S.C. § 901 et seq.) sets up a workers' compensation system for non-seaman maritime employees.

This Act covers longshoremen, harbor workers, shipbuilders, ship repairers, and dockworkers who work on navigable waters, piers, dry docks, and terminals.

The LHWCA provides medical coverage and wage replacement disability benefits without needing to prove employer fault.

In return for these benefits, Section 905(a) protects employers from personal injury lawsuits and claims for non-economic damages like pain and suffering.

However, Section 905(b) allows injured workers to file third-party negligence lawsuits against vessel owners if their operational negligence led to the injuries. LHWCA claims have a strict one-year statute of limitations.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA)

The OCSLA (43 U.S.C. § 1331 et seq.) regulates injuries on artificial islands, fixed production platforms, and structures permanently attached to the seabed beyond state waters.

Since fixed offshore platforms are not considered vessels under maritime law, permanent platform workers typically do not qualify as Jones Act seamen.

OCSLA uses the LHWCA framework to provide no-fault wage and medical benefits to platform workers.

For third-party personal injury lawsuits on fixed platforms, OCSLA applies the laws of the adjacent coastal state as surrogate federal law.

For instance, for injuries occurring off the Texas coast, Texas personal injury law applies, with a two-year statute of limitations for third-party claims.

Death on the High Seas Act (DOHSA)

If a fatal accident occurs beyond three nautical miles from U.S. shores, the Death on the High Seas Act (46 U.S.C. § 30301 et seq.) governs wrongful death claims.

DOHSA establishes a federal cause of action based on negligence or unseaworthiness.

However, damages in commercial maritime fatalities under DOHSA are limited to losses like lost income and household services.

Damages for emotional suffering or loss of companionship are not recoverable. DOHSA claims are subject to a three-year statute of limitations.

Defining Seaman Status: The Fifth Circuit's Restrictive Legal Test

Since Jones Act claims allow recovery for non-economic pain and suffering, proving seaman status is crucial in offshore litigation.

Following established Supreme Court guidelines, a worker must meet a two-part test to be classified as a Jones Act seaman:

The worker’s duties must contribute to the function of the vessel or help achieve its mission.

The worker must have a significant connection to a vessel in navigation (or a specific fleet) regarding both time and nature of work.

Federal courts in the Fifth Circuit enforce a 30 percent temporal requirement.

A worker who spends less than 30 percent of their time serving a vessel or fleet usually fails to meet the duration requirement and cannot claim Jones Act remedies.

In Sanchez v. Smart Fabricators of Texas, L.L.C., the Fifth Circuit determined that simply meeting the 30 percent threshold is not enough.

In Sanchez, a land-based welder injured on a jack-up rig did not qualify as a seaman because his duties were temporary and did not expose him to the usual risks of a mariner's job.

In Johnson v. Cooper T. Smith Stevedoring Co., the Fifth Circuit upheld a summary judgment against a longshoreman injured on a floating weigh station in the Mississippi River.

The court found that longshoremen assigned to transfer cargo daily are not permanent crew members and cannot bypass the exclusive remedy under LHWCA Section 905(a).

These rulings highlight that specialized contractors rotating between fixed platforms and jack-up rigs need thorough factual evaluation to determine their statutory coverage.

Vessel Classification Dynamics in Offshore Infrastructure

Whether an offshore structure is classified as a "vessel in navigation" affects the statutory remedies available under federal admiralty law.

Under 1 U.S.C. § 3 and the Supreme Court's Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach ruling, a structure is considered a vessel only if a reasonable observer would conclude it is designed primarily for transport on water.

Specialized floating equipment, such as mobile offshore drilling units (MODUs), jack-up drilling rigs, spud barges, floating cranes, and derrick barges, are legally defined as vessels.

These units keep their vessel status, even when stationary, secured to the ocean floor, or anchored during offshore operations.

In contrast, fixed production platforms permanently attached to the seabed are classified as non-vessels under OCSLA.

Permanently attached deepwater structures, including Tension Leg Platforms (TLPs), SPAR platforms, and permanently moored Floating Production Storage and Offloading (FPSO) units without active navigation capabilities, also qualify as non-vessels.

This classification standard applies to new energy sectors as well; federal decisions regarding offshore wind farm support vessels confirm that specialized installation craft remain subject to federal admiralty standards.

BSEE Regulatory Oversight and Offshore Safety Alerts

Safety data and incident investigations from the Bureau of Safety and Environmental Enforcement (BSEE) provide key evidence for identifying standard-of-care violations in offshore injury claims.

BSEE Safety Alert No. 505 found that slips, trips, and falls (STFs) made up 22 percent of reported injuries across Outer Continental Shelf assets, making them the leading cause of offshore accidents.

BSEE’s inspections at 19 Gulf facilities revealed major underlying issues:

Walkway Obstructions

Decks cluttered with temporary power lines, hoses, pipes, and scaffolding in emergency exit paths.

Lack of Hazard Audits

Failure to conduct formal "Start Work Checks" before high-pressure operations or lifting tasks.

Unguarded Structural Edges

Sharp, unprotected edges that led to serious cuts and puncture wounds from falls.

Simultaneous Operations Risks

Wet, oily deck surfaces combined with inattentive workers navigating active areas.

To address these risks, BSEE recommended practices like the "20-20-20 rule" (scanning 20 feet every 20 minutes) and strict adherence to Coast Guard non-slip footwear regulations under 33 CFR 142.33.

Follow-up BSEE safety alerts have highlighted broader risks across offshore facilities:

Equipment Misuse (Alerts 507 & 515)

Trends included workers using unapproved hand tools on live circuits and improper plugs during high-temperature welding.

Living Quarters Defects (Alert 513)

Severe corrosion caused exterior walkways to detach from Temporary Living Quarters (TLQs), posing fall risks over open water.

Electrical and Mechanical Fires (Alerts 514 & 518)

Skipping insulation testing led to seawater pump motor fires, while mechanical failures on transfer pumps caused flash fires.

Crane and Lifting Hazards (Alerts 512, 517 & 520): Corrosion issues.
