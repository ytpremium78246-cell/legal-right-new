State Healthcare Cost Recovery

Modern Frameworks in State Healthcare Cost Recovery: Subrogation, PBM Litigation, and Third-Party Liability Enforcement

State Healthcare Recovery PBM Litigation ERISA & Medicaid Subrogation
Legal Rights Center Practice Group March 2026
  • Published by Vanguard Health Law & Regulatory Policy Group

Executive Overview

State healthcare cost recovery has evolved from a simple administrative subrogation process to a dynamic and important area in public health litigation and regulatory enforcement. State attorneys general, Medicaid agencies, and public healthcare systems are facing tighter budgets and legal changes due to federal budget cuts and limits on healthcare payments. Consequently, states are increasingly filing civil lawsuits and using third-party liability mechanisms to recover billions in public healthcare spending.

This legal analysis examines the modern framework of state healthcare cost recovery. It looks at state subrogation rights, common law tort recovery, statutory Medicaid reimbursement enforcement, and complex lawsuits against pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) and pharmaceutical manufacturers.

As state budgets adjust to strict limits on State Directed Payments (SDPs) and federal oversight of public funding, cost-recovery litigation is an essential tool for helping state treasuries remain stable.

The Legal Foundations of State Healthcare Subrogation

State healthcare cost recovery relies on a two-part system

statutory subrogation and common law parens patriae authority. When a state spends public funds through Medicaid or employee health plans for injuries or illnesses caused by third parties, both federal and state laws require that the state seek reimbursement.

Under federal law (42 U.S.C. § 1396a(a)(25)), state Medicaid programs must make reasonable efforts to identify the legal responsibility of third parties for healthcare items and services paid for by Medicaid. States must create laws that automatically assign the beneficiary’s rights for medical support and payment to the state agency upon enrollment.

This assignment establishes a direct cause of action for the state against sources of third-party liability, including commercial insurers, tort settlement funds, product liability defendants, and companies whose illegal actions increased public healthcare costs.

Evolution of the Arkansas Dept. of Human Services v. Ahlborn Doctrine

A key legal challenge in state statutory cost recovery is limiting state subrogation liens to the portion of a tort settlement specifically assigned for medical expenses.

In Arkansas Department of Human Services v. Ahlborn (547 U.S. 268) and later in Gallardo v. Marstiller (596 U.S. 320), the Supreme Court clarified the reach of federal anti-lien provisions under 42 U.S.C. § 1396p(a)(1).

The Court decided that while states may seek reimbursement from settlement funds meant for medical expenses paid by Medicaid, federal law stops states from asserting liens against parts of a beneficiary’s recovery set aside for lost wages, pain and suffering, or future medical care.

When a third-party settlement does not clearly state damages, states may need to litigate allocation disputes in state courts or arrange administrative hearings to determine the exact recoverable share of past medical payments.

Affirmative Public Nuisance and Fraud Claims Against Corporate Entities

In addition to standard subrogation for personal injury claims, state healthcare cost recovery now includes large-scale lawsuits filed by state attorneys general.

Employing principles first developed during tobacco litigation and later used in opioid cases, states are making claims under state Consumer Protection Acts, Unfair and Deceptive Trade Practices Acts (UDTPA), and public nuisance laws to recover public healthcare funds.

In these cases, state plaintiffs argue that misleading marketing, price-fixing, or intentional over-prescribing changed clinical care standards, forcing state Medicaid and public employee health plans to manage large medical claims.

Since these claims are made directly by the state rather than through assignment from individual patients, states often bypass Ahlborn lien restrictions.

Instead, states pursue direct common law restitution, damages for unjust enrichment, and statutory civil penalties based on the total public funds misused.

PBM Cost Recovery: Rebate Retainage and Spread Pricing Litigation

Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM) recovery litigation is one of the fastest-growing areas in state healthcare cost recovery. States are actively pursuing PBMs to recover overpayments made by state Medicaid agencies and employee health insurance plans.

  • States are targeting three main PBM practices in litigation:

Spread Pricing Mechanisms

In spread pricing models, a PBM charges the state Medicaid managed care program a higher rate for a prescription drug than what it pays the pharmacy. The PBM keeps the difference as profit. State lawsuits allege that PBMs broke fiduciary duties, violated state contracts, and engaged in constructive fraud by manipulating drug pricing benchmarks like Average Wholesale Price (AWP) and National Average Drug Acquisition Cost (NADAC).

Rebate Concealment and Offshore Group Purchasing Organizations (GPOs)

To avoid laws that require drug rebates to be returned to state healthcare programs, major PBMs have set up offshore Group Purchasing Organizations (GPOs). States argue these offshore entities collect "administrative fees" and "rebate retention fees" from pharmaceutical manufacturers, effectively acting as hidden rebates. Lawsuits aim to recover these retained funds under state false claims acts and breach-of-contract theories.

Formulary Manipulation

States say PBMs often exclude lower-cost generic drugs, biosimilars, and affordable treatments from formularies in favor of higher-priced brand-name drugs that offer larger rebates for the PBM. This practice raises overall costs for state healthcare programs.

Following key federal regulatory actions and antitrust enforcement involving large PBMs, state attorneys general are using federal findings to negotiate recovery settlements and push for transparency in future state benefit contracts.

Preemption Challenges: The Intersection of ERISA and State Subrogation

A main legal defense used by private defendants in healthcare cost recovery lawsuits is federal preemption under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA), 29 U.S.C. § 1144(a).

When states enact laws to strengthen subrogation rights, regulate pharmacy reimbursements, or require transparency from third-party administrators, corporate defendants often argue these laws "relate to" employee benefit plans and are preempted by federal law.

State Subrogation Recovery Rules Apply

In ERISA’s framework:

The Express Preemption Clause (§ 514(a)) preempts all state laws that "relate to" any employee benefit plan.

The Savings Clause (§ 514(b)(2)(A)) preserves state laws that regulate insurance, banking, or securities from preemption.

The Deemer Clause (§ 514(b)(2)(B)) clarifies that self-insured ERISA plans cannot be "deemed" to be insurance companies, protecting self-insured plans from direct state regulation.

In important cases like Rutledge v. Pharmaceutical Care Management Association (592 U.S. 80), the U.S. Supreme Court defined ERISA preemption more narrowly. The Court ruled that state laws regulating drug pricing and third-party cost structures are general cost-regulation measures rather than laws governing plan administration.

This ruling strengthens state efforts to enforce cost recovery and drug pricing laws against third-party administrators and PBMs working within state borders.

Third-Party Liability Enforcement Under Modern Medicaid Budget Mandates

State healthcare recovery practices are becoming more complex due to federal budget regulations and statutory mandates. Federal policies that limit State Directed Payments (SDPs) and increase scrutiny on safety-net spending require state Medicaid programs to vigorously pursue all available third-party liability options before tapping into matching federal funds.
